Spanning millennia of human civilization, food has been a critical and everlasting part of the Roman and Asian (Indian & Chinese) experience, which despite their cultural differences share common ingredients and tastes. Cooking, therefore, is not only at the heart of home life, but that of any event from a family celebration to the largest national events or any form of entertainment; memories and fondness are tied to people’s bellies. In any festival or celebration — a positive event — food is always found!  The staple diet of any culture is based on the resources available and determined by topography, biome, and history. Rome was established in 753 BC/BCE near the Tiber River, the Apennine Mountains, and the Mediterranean Sea, resulting in the creation of the Mediterranean diet (Limbergen). The Romans simply prepared their food: if not eating it raw – which they commonly did – they boiled their vegetables and roasted their meat, keeping their meals as close to their natural state as possible to preserve their essence. Contrastingly, Eastern and Southern Asian cuisine diverged with frying and other forms of the melding of flavors. Some of these fusions resulted in: 宫保 or Kung Pao from China and पुलाव or Pulao from India. Asia, particularly China and India, have a variety of natural resources at their disposal.

 Not specified to one biome, the land was split into Mountainous, Desert, Tropical, Grassland, Forest, and Tundra (Teachers Domain). Each biome gave a specific set of resources and opportunities for the people who lived there. For example, the Tundra Biome, while it seems minimalist, yields abundant natural resources – like potable water – to the people living there (Teachers Domain). Ancient Romans did not like experimenting with their food and wanted to consume it fresh from the farm to the table. On the other hand, Asian cuisine was constructed from trial and error as the continent is gifted with bountiful resources hailing from varied biomes, allowing for experimentation with a panoply of flavors – particularly spice – creating dishes built upon one another. It can easily be argued that Asian cuisine conquered the ancient world’s kitchens and stomachs, including Rome’s, and inspired innovation in the culinary arts, with a more flavorful and well-balanced palate of dishes and continues to via social media platforms evolving further innovation and a curation of flavors.
Known for their immense desire to control the Grain Trade (which led to the Punic Wars), it is unsurprising that the Roman diet consisted of 65% cereals (Brown). Roman cereals included Durum Wheat, Barley, Einkorn (also known as Farro Monococcum), Millet, Oats, Purple Amaranth (also a Pseudocereal), Rye, and Rice (Brown). On the other hand, Asia predominantly used rice – a cereal grain – in their diets at a similar percentage to the Romans (Asia Society). Another large portion of the Roman diet was vegetables. Vegetables made up about 28% of their diet, both due to their need for cattle in agriculture (specifically in plowing) and religion (sacrifices to their gods, usually Mars and Jupiter), the Romans relied on a variety of vegetables for economics and long-employment (Brown, Grau-Sologestoa). Such staple crops included but were not limited to cabbage, onions, radish, cucumber, turnip, asparagus, kale, and lettuce (Brown). Unsurprisingly, the Romans did not eat a lot of spice, which was only about 0.1% of their sustenance, relying more heavily on what is modernly referred to as garden herbs such as basil, rosemary, sage, etc. (Brown). Connecting back to how food is part of the joy in a celebration, Roman festivals had an abundance of food. One of the most important of these festivals is Saturnalia, a celebration of Saturn (Kronos for the Greeks). This celebration had a large variety of food from roasted pig, fresh fruit, to Muslum, a drink made of wine and spices — like honey, pepper, and clove (Arbeia). When Alexander invaded India (he was unsuccessful in his conquest) he learned about the use of spice in Ayurvedic Medicine. Ayurvedic Medicine, a pivotal part of Indian medicine, is the use of herbs and spices to improve one's health. Some of these herbs and spices – which are common in Indian cuisine – are Amla, Turmeric, Arjuna, Neem, and Ashwagandha (Asian Inspirations).  Contrary to the Romans, more supplies are used in Asian celebrations. A vital celebration in Indian culture (specifically Hindus) is  Dussehra, or the festival marking the conquest of sunrise over darkness. The dishes in this festival include Matar Kachori (A potato and spice-filled pastry), Dahi Vada, Kheer, etc. These dishes are quite dependent on the resources available, so it’s hard to say what dish was made during the time.  The dishes created during a festival are dependent on numerous factors particularly topography, religion, weather (Season and Climate), resources available, and the health of the people. Certain seasons have specific balming foods, like drinking tea when having a cold. Similar to Rome, ancient Asia’s vegetables such as spring onion, bitter gourd, and eggplant all played a crucial role in their diet (Asia Society). Since Ancient India and China heavily influenced trade and culture throughout Asia, it is predictable that the ideas of each culture were passed on to other countries and empires. One of the most well-known similarities between Rome and Asia is the use of spice in both consumables and medicine. Although only the elites could afford spice (it had to be transported from Asia via the Silk Road) it became increasingly popular in ancient Roman cuisine if not for its flavor, but rather for its observed and impressed opulence. It can then be inferred that Asian cuisine is a progenitor to better Western cuisine standards and palate expansion of flavoring. Certainly, expectations for better-tasting foods became clear over time as trade routes became more defined and trade expedited via technological advancements in communications and transportation. The ancient Romans used a variety of spices, but the most common ones were: basil, black pepper, chili, cumin seeds, garlic, and honey. Correspondingly, Asia used these spices too, but the ones that were frequently used (in both dominant cultures) were turmeric, ginger, cinnamon, clove, chili, peppercorn, pepper, cardamom, and lastly star anise (Asian Inspirations). Pepper is common in both areas because of its prestige in the ancient world.  Today, due to factors like social media and immigration, culinary styles are being fused yet further. Euro-Asian cuisine, a fusion of European and Asian cuisine, is now popular in areas where migrants from Asia live (usually Singapore). The fusion of these two cuisines, is rather new, with examples like Assam Sauce, a sauce infusing Indian and Chinese culture while also using European ingredients, the use of garlic as a common factor to bridge the cultures.
Simply put, taste buds are ubiquitous and they like good tasting foods that are prepared with superior flavor profiles. Ingredients like spices, vegetables, and cereals are the base ingredients for both Asian and European cultures. These similarities did not happen by accident but by factors like historical trade routes, topography, and warfare. These similitudes illustrated a diverse culinary technique with a blend of traditions both foreign and domestic, local and from afar to these cultures, which implies that the Asian culinary technique improved their European counterparts. In the present, both culinary styles (particularly Asia) have influenced other cultures using modern methods, specifically the use of social media platforms. These modern “Silk Roads” allow these traditions to reach yet wider audiences; with videos and tutorials on creating such dishes more and more people can learn to appreciate and taste delicacies from cultures around the world, bridging the interpretation of other cultures (in a positive and delicious way)! However, if looked at strictly, these traditions could be eroded in the process of sharing, but that too would not be in keeping with the spirit of the Asian culinary traditions and recipes either in a way; quite the conundrum! Ultimately, despite the factors that separate them, Asian cuisine has influenced their European counterparts and thus the modern world, demonstrating that regardless of distance, people can learn from one another, and finally agree that food tastes better with flavor.
